\begin{abstract}
Adversarial training can improve robustness to bounded input perturbations, but
its robust test performance may peak and then degrade as training continues, a
phenomenon known as robust overfitting. This report studies how the perturbation
domain used during adversarial training affects that trajectory. We train
PreActResNet-18 models on CIFAR-10 for 200 epochs under pixel-only,
low-frequency-only, and epoch-wise mixed-domain projected gradient descent
(PGD) training. Each condition is evaluated across five seeds using clean,
pixel-PGD-20, low-frequency DCT-masked PGD-20, and per-example conjunction
metrics. Under pixel-only training, mean pixel robustness peaked at 51.22\%
at epoch 105 and fell by 8.56 percentage points by epoch 200. In contrast,
matched-domain robustness under low-frequency-only training declined by just
0.05 points from its 92.74\% peak, although pixel and conjunction accuracy
remained at 0.00\%. Mixed-domain training produced schedule-dependent
oscillations: mean pixel robustness was 42.36\% after pixel-training epochs
and 3.53\% after low-frequency-training epochs. These results show that the
observed robust-overfitting trajectory depends strongly on the perturbation
domain and that once-per-epoch random mixing yields domain-specialized states
rather than stable robustness across both evaluated attacks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Deep neural networks can be highly accurate on unmodified images while being
vulnerable to small, deliberately chosen perturbations
\cite{goodfellow2015,rice2020}. Adversarial training addresses this threat by
optimizing a model on adversarially perturbed examples. However, robust
performance need not improve monotonically: robust test accuracy can reach a
maximum during training and later decline even as the training objective
continues to improve \cite{rice2020,yu2022}. This gap between the best and
final robust checkpoint is the practical and empirical focus of this study.

The threat model is an important part of that story. Standard pixel-space PGD
can change every image coordinate within an $\linf$ budget, whereas a
frequency-restricted attack constrains its perturbation to smooth,
low-frequency patterns. Prior work links frequency content to robust features
and shows that robustness can trade off across heterogeneous perturbation
models \cite{guo2020,bu2023,tramer2019,maini2020}. It is therefore useful to
ask not only whether robustness overfits, but also under which evaluated threat
model it does so.

\paragraph{Research question.}
During PGD adversarial training of PreActResNet-18 on CIFAR-10, how does
randomly alternating pixel-space PGD and low-frequency DCT-masked PGD once per
epoch affect the timing and severity of robust overfitting, relative to
pixel-only and low-frequency-only training?

\paragraph{Hypothesis.}
With architecture, dataset, optimization schedule, perturbation budget, and
checkpoint-evaluation schedule held fixed, mixed-domain training will yield
robust-accuracy trajectories that differ from the single-domain conditions. In
particular, the peak epoch, peak shape, or post-peak decline may change under
pixel-space and/or low-frequency evaluation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This report makes the following scoped contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It implements and evaluates three controlled adversarial-training
  conditions using standard pixel PGD, DCT-masked low-frequency PGD, or a
  seeded epoch-wise choice between them.
  \item It tracks each saved checkpoint under clean, pixel-PGD-20,
  low-frequency-PGD-20, and per-example conjunction evaluation, rather than
  reporting only the threat model used for training.
  \item It characterizes robust-overfitting timing and best-versus-final
  decline over five independent seeds for each condition.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Adversarial examples and PGD adversarial training}
For a classifier $f_\theta$, input $x$, label $y$, and loss
$\ell(f_\theta(x),y)$, an $\linf$ adversary seeks a perturbation $\delta$
that increases loss while satisfying $\lVert\delta\rVert_\infty\leq\epsilon$.
Fast gradient sign attacks use a single gradient step; PGD repeats projected
gradient ascent, often from a random start, to form a stronger first-order
attack \cite{goodfellow2015,madry2018}. In adversarial training, the model is
optimized on such attacked examples rather than treating the attack only as a
post-hoc evaluation.

We use PGD-10 during training to keep the cost of adversarial-example
generation manageable across 200 epochs, while PGD-20 allocates more attack
iterations to the primary checkpoint evaluation. The additional iterations
are intended to provide a more thorough finite-step search under the same
perturbation budget, but they do not guarantee a worst-case perturbation or
certify robustness.

\subsection{Robust overfitting}
Rice et al.~\cite{rice2020} documented robust overfitting across data sets and
threat models: robust test performance can improve near a learning-rate change
and subsequently fall despite continued training. They emphasize selection of
an early checkpoint using a held-out validation set. Yu et al.~\cite{yu2022}
further connect robust overfitting to the treatment of small-loss adversarial
examples. In this report, robust overfitting is operationalized separately for
each evaluation metric as the difference between its best observed checkpoint
and its epoch-200 value; \cref{sec:analysis-plan} gives the precise definition.

\subsection{Frequency-restricted and multi-threat robustness}
The discrete cosine transform (DCT) represents images with spatial-frequency
coefficients. Restricting a perturbation to low-frequency coefficients produces
smooth, broad image changes \cite{guo2020}. Frequency-aware work suggests that
frequency components can make different contributions to prediction and robust
learning \cite{chen2022,bu2023}. Meanwhile, robustness against multiple
perturbation models can be imbalanced: a model trained for one threat may
remain weak for another \cite{tramer2019,maini2020}. Recent work also relates
heterogeneous threat models to distinct spectral signatures and possible
negative transfer \cite{xie2026}.

Tram\`er and Boneh's AVG and MAX strategies generate adversarial examples from
each threat model for every training input, then respectively train on all of
them or only on the example with the greatest loss \cite{tramer2019}. Maini
et al.'s Multi Steepest Descent (MSD) instead incorporates the threat-model
choice into the inner maximization by selecting the highest-loss candidate
direction at every attack step \cite{maini2020}. The mixed-domain condition in
this report does neither: a seeded random choice selects pixel-space or
low-frequency PGD once per epoch, and every batch in that epoch uses the
selected attack. It is therefore a stochastic alternation between
single-domain training objectives, not a per-example worst-case optimization
over the union of the two threat models.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Notation and threat models}
Images are $x\in[0,1]^{3\times32\times32}$ with labels $y\in\{1,\ldots,10\}$.
Let $\mathcal{B}_\infty(\epsilon)=\{\delta:\lVert\delta\rVert_\infty
\leq\epsilon\}$, where $\epsilon=8/255$. The pixel-space threat model allows
any $\delta\in\mathcal{B}_\infty(\epsilon)$. The low-frequency threat model
applies a two-dimensional DCT to the perturbation, retains an $8\times8$
top-left block of coefficients per channel, and transforms the result back to
pixel space. Thus it retains $64$ of the $1{,}024$ coefficients per channel
before the final image-range clipping step.

Let $C$ be the orthonormal DCT-II matrix and $M_8$ the binary low-frequency
mask. The low-frequency projection used in the implementation is
\begin{equation}
  P_8(\delta)=C^\top\bigl(M_8\odot(C\delta C^\top)\bigr)C.
  \label{eq:dct-projection}
\end{equation}
The code uniformly rescales projected perturbations when necessary to enforce
the $\linf$ budget, rather than clipping individual perturbation coordinates;
the resulting image is then clipped to $[0,1]$.

\subsection{Attack generation}
\label{sec:attacks}
For pixel-space PGD, the attack begins from a uniformly random perturbation in
the $\linf$ ball and iterates
\begin{equation}
  \delta_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}_\infty(\epsilon)}
  \left(\delta_t+\alpha\,\mathrm{sign}\left(\nabla_\delta
  \ell(f_\theta(x+\delta_t),y)\right)\right),
  \qquad \alpha=2/255.
  \label{eq:pixel-pgd}
\end{equation}
For low-frequency PGD, the random start and gradient direction are projected
with $P_8$. The projected direction is normalized by its per-example maximum
absolute value before its $\alpha$-sized update, then the perturbation is
uniformly rescaled if it exceeds $\epsilon$. Both attacks are white-box,
untargeted cross-entropy attacks.

The implementation checks in \cref{app:checks} confirm that the DCT
round-trip and masked projection are numerically accurate before final image
clipping. Clipping to $[0,1]$ does introduce nonzero out-of-mask energy: in a
256-image diagnostic low-frequency-PGD-20 run, its mean fraction was 0.70\%,
with a 95th percentile of 4.75\% and maximum of 17.56\%. We therefore use
``DCT-masked low-frequency PGD'' to describe the attack-generation procedure,
not a claim that every final, image-clipped perturbation is exactly
band-limited. The final perturbations remained within the stated $\linf$
budget to floating-point precision.

\label{sec:conditions}

and $\linf$ budget. The only intended treatment difference is the attack domain
used to generate adversarial training examples.

\section{Evaluation and Analysis Plan}
\label{sec:analysis-plan}

\subsection{Checkpoint evaluation}
Each saved checkpoint is evaluated on the full CIFAR-10 test set under four
quantities:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item clean accuracy, without an attack;
  \item pixel robust accuracy, after pixel-space PGD-20;
  \item low-frequency robust accuracy, after DCT-masked low-frequency PGD-20;
  \item conjunction accuracy, where an image counts as correct only if it is
  correctly classified after both independently generated attacks.
\end{enumerate}
Cross-entropy loss is additionally recorded for clean, pixel-PGD, and
low-frequency-PGD inputs. Values in the overall CSVs are the arithmetic mean
and sample standard deviation across five seeds at each checkpoint.

\paragraph{Important terminology.}
The code's fourth quantity is a per-example \emph{conjunction} of correctness
under two separately generated attacks. It is a useful strict summary, but it
is not itself a single adaptive optimization over the union of threat sets.
Because a low-frequency, $\linf$-bounded perturbation is structurally
restricted relative to the pixel $\linf$ threat set, this comparison also
requires care: finite-step PGD measurements should not be interpreted as a
certificate of set-wise robustness. This distinction should be retained in the
final report and captions.

\subsection{Robust-overfitting measures}
Let $A_m(e)$ denote mean test accuracy for metric $m$ at evaluated checkpoint
epoch $e\in\E=\{5,10,\ldots,200\}$. Define
\begin{align}
  e_m^* &= \underset{e\in\E}{\arg\max}\;A_m(e), \\
  \mathrm{ROG}_m &= A_m(e_m^*)-A_m(200),
  \label{eq:robust-overfitting-gap}
\end{align}
where ROG denotes the robust-overfitting gap in percentage points. Ties must
be resolved using a predeclared rule; this draft uses the earliest evaluated
epoch. Aggregate peaks and ROGs in \cref{tab:main-results} are calculated
from the condition mean trajectory, whereas \cref{app:per-seed} reports each
seed's own earliest peak. The five-seed summaries use mean $\pm$ sample
standard deviation descriptively; this study does not report confidence
intervals or formal hypothesis tests.

\subsection{Planned reporting checks}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Report means and standard deviations across seeds, plus individual
  seed trajectories in an appendix or supplement.
  \item Keep model selection separate from test reporting: the test curve is
  descriptive here and must not be presented as an unbiased validation-based
  early-stopping procedure.
  \item An attack-strength diagnostic is reported in \cref{app:checks}. It
  compares the primary single-restart PGD-20 attack with a three-restart
  PGD-50 attack on two selected pixel-only checkpoints. It is a limited check,
  not a comprehensive robustness evaluation or certificate.
  \item Uncertainty is descriptive only: mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation
  summarizes the five seeds, without a preregistered or inferential test.
\end{itemize}

The results therefore answer the research question descriptively: the observed

0.05 percentage points after its aggregate peak. The mixed condition displays

\subsection{Pixel-only control}
\label{sec:pixel-results}
The pixel-only control displays the expected signature of robust overfitting
under pixel-PGD-20: aggregate pixel robustness peaks at epoch 105 and falls by
8.56 percentage points, from 51.22\% to 42.66\%, by epoch 200. Every seed
reaches its individual pixel-robustness peak at epoch 105. Low-frequency
robustness peaks later, at 78.36\% at epoch 155, and ends at 76.48\%, a smaller
1.88-point decline. Mean pixel robust loss reaches its minimum of 1.291 at
epoch 105 and rises to 3.223 by epoch 200, while the final clean accuracy
remains 84.40\%. Pixel and conjunction accuracy are effectively identical
throughout this condition, indicating that pixel-PGD-20 is the binding one of
the two attacks under this evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Low-frequency-only condition}
\label{sec:low-frequency-results}
The low-frequency-only condition produces high and stable matched-domain
accuracy. Mean low-frequency robustness reaches 92.74\% at epoch 195 and ends
at 92.70\%, a decline of only 0.05 percentage points; its final clean accuracy

every evaluated checkpoint for every seed. Low-frequency robust loss reaches
its minimum of 0.266 at epoch 105 and ends at 0.318. Thus, under these
finite-step evaluations, robustness learned against the restricted attack
does not transfer to the pixel-space attack.

\subsection{Mixed-domain condition}
\label{sec:mixed-results}
The mixed-domain condition yields an apparent aggregate pixel-robustness peak
of 40.80\% and conjunction peak of 40.79\% at epoch 85. By epoch 200, these
metrics fall to 19.33\% and 19.33\%, respectively, corresponding to raw gaps
of 21.47 and 21.46 percentage points. These gaps are confounded by the
epoch-wise attack schedule. All five seeds used pixel training at epoch 85,
whereas only seed 42 used pixel training at epoch 200. Across the 200
seed--checkpoint observations, mean pixel robustness is 42.36\% immediately
after pixel-training epochs and 3.53\% after low-frequency-training epochs;
the point-biserial correlation between the most recent domain and pixel
robustness is $r=0.971$. Moreover, individual seeds reach their conjunction
peaks between epochs 115 and 160, not at the aggregate peak of epoch 85.

This recency effect also accounts for the large epoch-200 variation:
pixel-PGD-20 and conjunction accuracy have sample standard deviations of
14.28 and 14.27 percentage points. Low-frequency robustness is higher and
more stable in aggregate, peaking at 86.92\% at epoch 195 and ending at
85.20\%. The mixed condition therefore demonstrates alternation between
domain-specialized states, rather than providing evidence that conventional
robust overfitting alone caused the raw 21.46-point conjunction gap.

\subsection{Cross-condition comparison}
\label{sec:cross-condition}
The comparison reveals a trade-off between matched-domain performance and
measured coverage across attacks. Pixel-only training attains the highest
final measured pixel and conjunction robustness, but both metrics peak at
epoch 105 and decline by 8.56 percentage points. The
low-frequency-only condition attains the highest final clean and
low-frequency robustness and shows no practically meaningful matched-domain
overfitting, but it never correctly classifies a test example under both
attacks. Thus, strong robustness to the restricted low-frequency attack does
not by itself provide robustness to pixel-PGD-20.

Epoch-wise mixed training does not resolve this trade-off. Its final mean
clean and low-frequency accuracies lie between the two single-domain
conditions, but its pixel and conjunction results vary sharply with the most
recent training domain. Unlike the pixel-only condition, whose five seeds all
peak at the same epoch and have tightly clustered final accuracies, the mixed
condition's aggregate peak does not represent the individual seeds' peak
epochs and its final pixel standard deviation reaches 14.28 points. Under the
protocol studied here, the frequency restriction therefore changes both the
apparent robust-overfitting trajectory and cross-domain transfer, while
once-per-epoch random mixing produces rapid specialization rather than stable
joint robustness. These conclusions are limited to the specified PGD-20
measurements and do not establish robustness against adaptive or stronger
attacks.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation}
The present trajectories are consistent with robust overfitting being
evaluation-domain dependent in this experimental setting: pixel robustness
shows a sizable best-versus-final gap in the pixel-only and mixed-domain
aggregates, while low-frequency robustness declines less or remains highest at
the end of training. The experiments alone do not identify why this happens.
Possible explanations involving frequency bias, optimization dynamics, or
negative transfer should be presented as hypotheses grounded in prior work,
not as causal findings.

\subsection{Practical implications}
The control condition suggests that the final checkpoint can be substantially
worse than an earlier checkpoint under pixel-PGD-20, reinforcing the practical
importance of validation-based early stopping. The low-frequency-only condition
also illustrates that strong performance under a restricted perturbation model
does not imply observed robustness to unrestricted pixel PGD. For a deployment
claim, model selection and evaluation must match the intended threat model.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Attack evaluation.} PGD-20 with one reported configuration is
  not a proof of robustness. Stronger steps, restarts, adaptive attacks, and
  broader attack suites may change measured accuracy.
  \item \textbf{Conjunction versus union attack.} The conjunction metric is
  calculated after two independently generated attacks; it is not an adaptive
  inner maximization over their union. This must not be labeled an exact
  worst-case union robustness result.
  \item \textbf{Nested threat structure.} With the same $\linf$ budget, the
  low-frequency construction is a restricted perturbation family before
  image-range clipping. Differences between finite-step attacks therefore need
  an attack-optimization interpretation as well as a threat-family one.
  \item \textbf{Scope.} The study uses one architecture, CIFAR-10, a single
  cutoff ($8$), one 200-epoch schedule, and five seeds. It does not establish
  generality across data sets, architectures, budgets, or frequency bands.
  \item \textbf{Mixed-treatment granularity.} The mixed method alternates at
  epoch level and has no explicit balancing constraint on the realized count of
  domains, no per-example worst-case selection, and no gradient-compatibility
  measurement.
  \item \textbf{Model selection.} Checkpoint peaks are found on the test set
  for descriptive analysis; they are not validation-selected estimates of
  deployable early-stopping performance.

  environment uncertainty, or deviations from intended training commands.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Attack-Strength and Implementation Checks}
\label{app:checks}
All checks in this appendix use a fixed, approximately class-balanced
256-image subset of the CIFAR-10 test set (selection seed 20260912). They are
diagnostic checks only; the primary results remain the full-test-set,
single-restart PGD-20 measurements reported in \cref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{DCT and perturbation checks.}
On eight random $32\times32$ tensors, the maximum absolute DCT--inverse-DCT
round-trip error was $1.11\times10^{-5}$. Projecting these tensors to the
$8\times8$ mask left at most $2.53\times10^{-12}$ of DCT energy outside the
mask. We then generated low-frequency PGD-20 examples with the
low-frequency-only seed-42 epoch-200 checkpoint. Before final image clipping,
the maximum out-of-mask energy fraction was $3.36\times10^{-12}$; after
clipping, the mean, 95th-percentile, and maximum fractions were 0.70\%,
4.75\%, and 17.56\%, respectively. The maximum final perturbation norm was
$0.03137258$, equal to $8/255$ to floating-point precision. Thus projection
and budget enforcement behave as intended, while final image clipping creates
some nonzero high-frequency leakage that should be retained in the
threat-model caveat.

The stronger attack reduces accuracy by one example out of 256 at each
checkpoint and raises mean cross-entropy loss from 1.312 to 1.319 at epoch 105
and from 3.486 to 3.529 at epoch 200. This small diagnostic does not reveal a

substitute for multiple full-test restarts, adaptive attacks, or a broader
attack suite.
